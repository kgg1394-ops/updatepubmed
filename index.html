💡 Conclusion
Immunotherapy is a type of treatment that helps the body’s immune system find and attack cancer cells. In colorectal (bowel) cancer that has spread to other parts of the body, it works well for only a small group of patients. Their tumors have a fault in the way they repair their DNA. Most bowel cancers do not have this fault, and immunotherapy alone does little for them. Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy is a very precise type of radiation treatment. It gives a high dose of radiation to a tumor while sparing the healthy tissue around it. Laboratory studies suggest it may do more than kill cancer cells. Dying cancer cells may send out signals that wake up the immune system. In some cases, this may help shrink tumors that were not treated with radiation. In this review, we looked at whether giving this type of radiation together with immunotherapy could help people with bowel cancer that has spread. So far, studies show the combination is generally safe. But these studies are small, and none has yet shown that adding radiation to immunotherapy helps people live longer. Larger studies are needed to find out which patients are most likely to benefit and how best to combine the two treatments. Until then, this approach should mainly be used in clinical trials.
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are effective in the approximately 5% of metastatic colorectal cancers (mCRC) that are microsatellite instability-high or mismatch repair-deficient (MSI-H/dMMR), whereas microsatellite-stable or mismatch repair-proficient (MSS/pMMR) disease rarely responds to ICI monotherapy. Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) has been proposed to prime antitumor immunity in MSS disease. Preclinical studies show that high-dose-per-fraction radiation can induce immunogenic cell death, release tumor antigens and activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway, which may increase T-cell infiltration and, in some models, cause regression of non-irradiated lesions (the abscopal effect). Clinical data come mainly from small, single-arm, early-phase studies that report acceptable safety but modest response rates, and no colorectal-specific randomized trial has shown a survival benefit from adding SABR to immunotherapy. This review summarizes the biological rationale, critically appraises the clinical evidence, including chemo-immuno-radiotherapy in rectal cancer, and discusses toxicity, patient selection, dose-fractionation and sequencing. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from January 2000 to February 2026. SABR-induced immune priming in MSS mCRC is biologically plausible but should be tested in biomarker-stratified randomized trials.

💡 Conclusion
IgG4-related disease is a chronic, immune-mediated, systemic condition that presents as tumour-like masses. Since publication of the last international consensus statement on the treatment of IgG4-related disease in 2015, the related literature has expanded considerably, with two phase 3, randomised, placebo-controlled trials published within the past 2 years. This foundation of evidence for treatment decisions justified new, dedicated treatment guidelines. 62 investigators-comprising 52 physicians, one medical reference librarian, eight patients, and one caregiver from 15 countries across six continents-were assembled to form a treatment guidelines group. The physicians had a broad range of specialties and patient representation spanned the full spectrum of organ involvement. The treatment guidelines group generated 183 candidate questions, which were consolidated into 40 final questions approved by the IgG4ward! Advisory Board. These questions formed the basis of a systematic literature search, led by a medical reference librarian, which informed the development of 28 treatment statements. Supporting evidence for each statement was independently graded using the 2011 Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence framework. The 52 physicians voted electronically on their level of agreement with each statement; consensus required more than 80% to agree or strongly agree with the statement, and was reached for 22 of the 28 statements. The evidence base reflects marked recent progress due to the publication of phase 3 clinical trials, although most statements still rely on low-level evidence. This effort provides cohesive, consensus-based guidelines from a geographically and professionally diverse group of experts in IgG4-related disease, reflecting the evolution in the field since 2015. Despite this progress, important knowledge gaps remain, and are highlighted to guide future research.

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched for studies directly comparing hinotori with da Vinci robotic surgery in patients with colorectal cancer. Study-level data were pooled using random-effects models, with mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes and odds ratios (ORs) for dichotomous outcomes, each with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To identify critical clinical predictors of CRC risk in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) using machinelearning (ML) and to subsequently evaluate their causal relationship with CRC via Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.

A retrospective cohort design was adopted, analyzing clinical records from patients with T2DM and CRC and from those with T2DM alone. Management depends on aneurysm size, symptoms, morphology, anatomical relationships, comorbidities, and rupture status.

Case 1 was a 66-year-old man with chronic liver disease and thrombocytopenia in whom a giant true SAA measuring approximately 72 × 68 × 76 mm was incidentally identified during trauma imaging. Approximately 30% of patients develop liver-confined recurrences after local treatment for colorectal liver metastases (CRLM), which may be reduced by adjuvant therapy. Hepatic arterial infusion pump (HAIP) chemotherapy delivers high-dose liver-directed chemotherapy.

To investigate adjuvant HAIP chemotherapy without systemic therapy in patients with resectable liver-confined recurrence after prior local treatment of CRLM.

This multicenter, single-arm phase II trial enrolled patients with resectable recurrent CRLM across 3 centers in the Netherlands. Patients with prior or present extrahepatic disease were excluded. Adjuvant HAIP with floxuridine was administered up to 6 cycles. The primary endpoint was hepatic disease-free survival (hDFS) estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with a prespecified efficacy boundary of 36 months.

Forty-five patients underwent local treatment followed by adjuvant HAIP chemotherapy without systemic therapy. Twenty-eight patients(62%) were treated for a first recurrent CRLM, and 17 patients for a second or higher. Twenty-five patients (56%) had ≥3 CRLM. All patients started HAIP chemotherapy, with a median of 6 cycles (interquartile range: 4 to 6). After a median follow-up of 38.7 months (95% CI: 33.0-42.3), the median hDFS was 16.5 months (95% CI: 9.1-25.8). The median progression-free survival was 14.0 months (95% CI: 7.7-23.0), and the 3-year overall survival was 71% (95% CI: 58%-87%). Initial recurrence included the liver in 23 patients (51%). HAIP implantation-related complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3) occurred in 4 patients (9%), and HAIP chemotherapy-related complications (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade ≥3) occurred in 6 patients (13%). Biliary sclerosis occurred in 4 patients; 3 required stent placement.

Adjuvant HAIP chemotherapy without systemic therapy did not achieve the hypothesized hDFS in patients undergoing repeat local treatment for recurrent CRLM.
