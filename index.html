To identify the types of cancer and the corresponding screening methods evaluated in randomized controlled trials (RCTs), with a primary focus on situating oral cancer screening within the RCT-based body of evidence.

This review followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, LILACS, ProQuest, and Google Scholar were searched without date restrictions for studies in English, Spanish, or Portuguese. Eligible studies were RCTs primarily evaluating cancer screening methods (clinical, imaging, endoscopic, laboratory, molecular/serological, or self-examination). Data were synthesized as means and frequencies.

Nine cancer types and 33 distinct screening methods were identified across 97 cancer screenings in 91 RCTs spanning 57 years. Oral cancer ranked last, with only one RCT evaluating a single method, oral visual inspection, the fewest of any cancer type. By comparison, colorectal cancer was the most frequently investigated (n = 22/22.7%), followed by breast cancer (n = 21/21.6%) and lung cancer (n = 18/18.6%), with five, six, and four screening methods evaluated, respectively.

These findings map the RCT landscape in cancer screening, revealing marked disparities in evidence across cancer types and identifying oral cancer as the most limited. Further RCTs are urgently needed to strengthen the evidence base and support effective oral cancer screening programs.

To map the systemic and oral adverse events (AEs) of semaglutide reported in randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

This scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Only english-language and RCTs were considered.

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science from 2019 until 2025.

Of 630 identified records (300 in PubMed, 312 in Scopus, and 18 in Web of Science), 12 studies were included after screening. The review identified systemic AEs of semaglutide, primarily gastrointestinal events like nausea (12 studies) and diarrhea (11 studies). Less frequent effects included pancreatitis (5 studies), hepatobiliary (3 studies) and gallbladder disease (3 studies), dental caries (1 study) and dry mouth (1 study). Subcutaneous administration predominating (7 studies), followed by oral (3) and mixed routes (2), over treatment durations of 21-68 weeks and 4-9 weeks of follow-up. Additionally, systemic events such as nasopharyngitis (5 studies), COVID-19 (5 studies), and metabolic or nutrition-related disorders were reported in all 12 studies. Importantly, none of the included studies directly investigated oral or dental outcomes.

Semaglutide demonstrated an overall favorable safety profile in the included randomized controlled trials, with adverse events predominantly gastrointestinal. However, the RCTs did not provide direct evidence regarding oral health outcomes. Therefore, the available evidence is insufficient to draw conclusions about oral adverse events associated with semaglutide, highlighting the need for randomized clinical trials specifically designed to assess oral health outcomes in patients receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists.

💡 Conclusion
Current clinical evidence does not justify selecting individual anesthetic agents solely for oncological benefit. Nevertheless, combining tailored pharmacological strategies, specifically opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, with meticulous physiological optimization into a structured 'oncoanesthesia care bundle' provides a biologically coherent, low-risk framework available for immediate implementation. Future oncological trials must shift from pragmatic pan-cancer designs toward large-scale, patient-, cancer-, and gene-expression-specific trial protocols to truly capture personalized oncological outcomes.
Lung cancer surgery creates a vulnerable perioperative window where anesthetic and physiological interventions may modulate recurrence. This review evaluates the oncological impact of perioperative strategies in non-small-cell lung cancer, proposing a comprehensive 'oncoanesthesia care bundle'.

Current clinical data expose a persistent gap between compelling preclinical rationales and observable clinical outcomes. The recent General Anesthetics in CAncer REsection Surgery (2026), a large-scale evaluation of propofol versus volatile anesthesia, enrolled mixed cancer cohorts and demonstrated no oncological advantage of propofol, underscoring that the ongoing Inhalational Gas Anesthesia and Propofol-based Total IntraVenous Anesthesia trial will provide the first adequately powered, lung cancer-specific evaluation. Similarly, prospective randomized data regarding regional anesthesia have failed to show a significant recurrence benefit in lung cancer surgery. For nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, the Adjuvant Low-dose ASpirin in Colorectal CAncer trial's recent biomarker-driven success in colorectal cancer highlights the urgent necessity for analogous molecularly tailored investigations in non-small-cell lung cancer. Beyond pharmacological agents, meticulous physiological optimization remains critical: lung-protective ventilation, restrictive-to-euvolemic fluid management, and maintaining normothermia are biologically coherent strategies that preserve the endothelial glycocalyx, optimize interstitial fluid pressure, and maintain natural killer cell function.

Current clinical evidence does not justify selecting individual anesthetic agents solely for oncological benefit. Nevertheless, combining tailored pharmacological strategies, specifically opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, with meticulous physiological optimization into a structured 'oncoanesthesia care bundle' provides a biologically coherent, low-risk framework available for immediate implementation. Future oncological trials must shift from pragmatic pan-cancer designs toward large-scale, patient-, cancer-, and gene-expression-specific trial protocols to truly capture personalized oncological outcomes.

The incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer (eoCRC; <50 years) is rising. Previous studies suggested that eoCRC patients have poorer outcomes than older adults. Whether this reflects distinct tumor biology or differences in stage at diagnosis and treatment patterns remains unclear. We compared clinicopathological features, treatment, and survival of eoCRC with average-onset (ao) and older-onset (oo)CRC in a nationwide cohort.

Clinicopathological data of all patients diagnosed with colorectal adenocarcinoma in the Netherlands from 1 January 2016 through 31 December 2022 were retrieved from the Netherlands Cancer Registry (NCR). Patients were categorized as eoCRC, aoCRC (50-69 years), and ooCRC (≥70 years). Multivariable regression and matched analyses were performed to account for established prognostic factors and predictors of treatment response.

Among 80,551 patients with CRC, 3917 (5%) were eoCRC, 34,499 (43%) were aoCRC, and 42,135 (52%) were ooCRC. EoCRC patients more often presented with advanced disease (stage III or IV) and left-sided colon or rectal cancer. Five-year relative survival (RS) for eoCRC, aoCRC and ooCRC patients was 70%, 77%, and 69%, respectively (crude excess hazard ratio [EHR] for eoCRC vs. aoCRC 1·32 [95% CI 1·24-1·41]). In patients with mismatch repair proficient (pMMR) stage IV disease, eoCRC was associated with better RS than aoCRC (EHR 0·87 [95% CI 0·80-0·94]), concurrent with more intensive first-line systemic therapy. After matching for disease characteristics and treatment, RS was similar between eoCRC and aoCRC (EHR 1·02 [95% CI 0·91-1·14]), whereas ooCRC patients retained worse survival.

The poorer crude survival observed in eoCRC is largely associated with more advanced disease at diagnosis, rather than evidence of intrinsically distinct tumor biology. After accounting for established prognostic factors and factors predicting treatment response, survival of eoCRC and aoCRC patients is similar, challenging the notion that eoCRC constitutes a separate biological disease entity.
